Setting the Table

The Long Argument of Halakhah

Jewish law began as a 2,700-page argument. This is the story of the thousand-year relay race — through Baghdad, Fez, Troyes, Toledo, Kraków — that turned that argument into a book the whole Jewish world could open. And of the two people, a Spanish exile and a Polish prodigy, who together changed the Jewish world without ever meeting.

IThe problem

A library that refuses simple answers

Halakhah is often translated “Jewish law,” but the word itself means walking. It is less a rulebook than a record of a people deciding, step by step and generation by generation, how a life should be walked: how to eat, rest, grieve, forgive, and begin again. Inside its arguments about ritual and food live older questions: What do we owe each other? What is sacred enough to guard?

What looks like a legal library is a civilization’s long meditation on how to live well. But its foundational document has a famous personality problem: it doesn’t like easy verdicts.

The Talmud, completed in Babylonia around the year 500, is not a code of law. It is a record of argument — centuries of rabbis questioning, objecting, proving, and telling stories, with the disagreements lovingly preserved. Ask the Talmud a simple question — can I eat this? is this contract valid? — and it will often hand you three opinions, a counter-argument, a digression about a rabbi’s dream, and no ruling.

That’s a beautiful way to preserve a tradition of thinking. It’s a terrible way to run a community. A butcher in the year 900 with a questionable cow standing in front of him cannot wait for the debate to resolve itself. Somebody has to decide.

The engine of this whole story Every generation after the Talmud faced the same tension: fidelity to the argument versus the need for an answer. Lean too far toward answers and you flatten a living tradition into a pamphlet. Lean too far toward argument and nobody knows what to do. Everyone you’re about to meet is taking a position on this dilemma — and their positions sort, remarkably neatly, by geography.

One reading tip before we start: rabbis in this story are usually known by acronyms of their names, the way Americans say “JFK” or “RBG.” Rambam = Rabbi Moshe ben Maimon. Rashi = Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki. We’ll decode each one as it arrives. And sometimes the book eats the person: a rabbi can become so identified with his masterpiece that its title simply becomes his name — you’ll meet “the Tur” and “the Levush,” both people known by their books.

II600–1038 · Babylonia

The first deciders: the Geonim

For its first five centuries, halakhah had a capital city — really two: the great academies of Sura and Pumbedita in Babylonia (modern Iraq), the same institutions that had produced the Talmud itself. (There had once been a third great center, Nehardea — the most ancient of them all — destroyed in war back in 259 CE; its refugee scholars had founded Pumbedita.)

Two academies and their Excellencies

Each academy was led by a Gaon — “Excellency” — and for four hundred years the Geonim were, in effect, the supreme court of the Jewish world. Communities from Spain to Egypt sent them questions by caravan and ship; the answers came back as responsa — question-and-answer legal rulings, thousands of which survive. The Geonim also produced the first attempts to distill the Talmud into something usable: early digests like the She’iltot (8th century) and the Halakhot Gedolot (9th century), and, from the towering Saadia Gaon (882–942), the first works organizing law by topic.

Life inside the academies

A few glimpses inside those academies, because they’re wonderfully strange to modern eyes. The Talmud there was studied — and largely known — by heart: an elite oral literature, recited in rows ranked by ability, never meant for wide circulation. The Geonim saw themselves not as some interpreters among many but as the interpreters, heirs in an unbroken chain to the rabbis of the Talmud itself. And they invented the first rules for taming it — formal principles for deciding whose opinion wins when the Talmud leaves an argument open. Every code in this story stands on those rules.

Answers that became institutions

Their answers could become institutions. When a community in Spain sent the deceptively simple question “What is the order of the prayers?”, Rav Amram Gaon wrote back a complete annotated liturgy — and that responsum, copied and recopied, became the ancestor of the siddur (prayer book). Another Gaon, Sherira, answered a question about where the Mishnah came from with a fifteen-thousand-word history of the whole tradition that scholars leaned on for the next nine hundred years.

Meanwhile, in the caliph’s court

One more piece of context worth knowing: the Geonim weren’t working in a vacuum. In the very same city and centuries, Islamic law was going through its own formative struggles — with striking parallels in methods, genres, even the same anxieties. (When a Persian courtier proposed to the caliph in the 750s that the sprawling, contradictory legal tradition be simplified into one clear code, he was executed for it. The tension between the living argument and the tidy code was in the Baghdad air itself.)

It’s no accident that this arrangement flourished when it did. The academies sat at the center of the Abbasid Caliphate — an Islamic empire stretching from Spain to India, with Baghdad as the hub of its trade routes, its coinage, and its intellectual life. The Geonim’s worldwide reach rode on the caliphate’s roads and ships. And when the caliphate began to fragment in the 900s — rival dynasties in Egypt, an outright rival caliphate declared in Córdoba in 929 — the Jewish world fragmented right along with it. Wealthy new communities in Muslim Spain and North Africa began cultivating scholars of their own, and stopped waiting for answers from Baghdad.

Notice what made the system work: everyone agreed on who was in charge. One center, one address for hard questions. It could not last. Baghdad’s political decline dragged the academies down with it, and when the brilliant Hai Gaon died in 1038 with no successor of his stature, the era ended. The money and the questions had already been flowing elsewhere — to new communities along the Mediterranean and up into Europe.

The hinge moment After 1038, Judaism never again had a single legal capital. Authority scattered into regional centers that developed their own texts, teachers, customs — and temperaments. The rest of this story is about what those regions built, and how, five hundred years later, two of the traditions were stitched back together on a single page.

The handover

You can watch the handover happen in the books. Almost the moment Baghdad falls silent, the first systematic commentaries on the whole Talmud appear — nearly simultaneously, at opposite ends of the Mediterranean: Rabbenu Chananel in Kairouan (North Africa) and the school of Rabbenu Gershom in Mainz (Germany). The meaning is unmistakable: communities that can no longer write to the Gaon are taking the Talmud into their own hands — and they need guides. That is the world into which our next two heroes are born.

IIIThe map

The new map: many worlds

After Baghdad, no single address replaced Babylonia. Jewish life scattered into many worlds — Yemen, Italy, Byzantium, Persia, and more, each with its own story. This site follows the zones where the legal relay ran hottest, and gives each one a colour that will track it across every map and page to come.

Sefarad

Sefarad is simply the Hebrew name for the Iberian Peninsula — borrowed from a place-name in the book of Obadiah — and “Sephardic” Jews are the heirs of that world. Shaped first under Muslim rule (Arabic-speaking, philosophical, courtly) and then under the Christian kingdoms of the Reconquista, Iberian Jewry prized clarity and decision: the great codes of this story are its signature export.

Sefarad

“Give me the bottom line.”Spain and North Africa, under Islamic rule for centuries. Cosmopolitan, philosophical, at home with Arabic science and systematic thinking. Sephardic scholars loved codes: organize the law, cut the debate, state the ruling.

Ashkenaz — a family of communities

Ashkenaz is the Hebrew name medieval Jews attached to Germany — borrowed from a name in Genesis’s table of nations — and “Ashkenazi” Jews are that family’s descendants. Historically, Germany and northern France were closely intertwined sibling centres — shared founders, constant movement of students — that nonetheless developed distinct customs; “Ashkenaz” narrowly means Germany, and only later stretched to cover the whole family. Living as small communities inside Christian Europe, often under pressure, they prized inherited custom and fearless analysis over tidy codes. Poland is where the family moved when its old homes became unlivable.

Germany

“Our custom is itself the law.”The Rhineland towns — Mainz, Worms, Speyer. Small, pious, tightly knit communities in Christian Europe, repeatedly battered by Crusader massacres. Ashkenaz treated inherited custom as sacred: what our martyred ancestors did is the tradition.

France

“Show me the argument.”Germany’s sibling culture, centered in Champagne and northern France. Its genius was commentary and dialectic — explaining the Talmud line by line, then arguing with the explanation. Answers mattered less than understanding why.

Poland

“Ashkenaz, act two.”As German Jewry was decimated by the massacres around the Black Death (1348–49), the centre of Ashkenazi life moved east into Poland–Lithuania, which by the 1500s hosted Europe’s largest Jewish community and its most electric academies.

The connectors

Two zones that belonged fully to neither camp — and kept the whole conversation connected.

Provence

“We read everyone — and grade them.”Southern France: geographically and intellectually between the worlds. Provençal scholars translated Sephardic philosophy for Europe, absorbed both traditions, and produced the era’s sharpest critics.

Babylonia & the East

“The source.”Where it all started — and, after 1492, where much of it ended up: the Ottoman Empire, and above all the little Galilean hill town of Tzfat, welcomed the Spanish exiles.

One origin story is worth pausing on, because it explains so much of what follows. The founding families of Ashkenaz came up from Italy — invited north as merchants by Charlemagne’s successors — and they carried with them ancient customs rooted in the old Land-of-Israel tradition, which often differed from the Babylonian Talmud’s rulings. When the Babylonian Talmud swept Europe and became the standard, those cherished older practices weren’t abandoned; they were promoted to the newly sacred status of minhag — binding local custom. Ashkenaz’s famous reverence for custom, in other words, isn’t stubbornness. It’s the community’s oldest inheritance, protected from its own new rulebook — and, after the Crusader massacres you’ll meet shortly, sanctified by martyrdom too.

The story of halakhah moves when people are forced to move, and its greatest syntheses were written by refugees; drag the slider (or tap the arrows) through five eras and watch the centre of gravity travel from Baghdad to Kraków and Tzfat. Tap any city for its story.

IVc. 1000–1200 · Fez, Troyes

Two ways to tame the Talmud

With Babylonia gone, two men born a generation apart — one at each end of the Mediterranean world — invented the two rival strategies that would define everything after them. Same problem, opposite instincts.

Strategy 1: Shrink it

The Rif (Rabbi Isaac Alfasi, 1013–1103) of Fez, Morocco — later a refugee in Spain — went through the entire Talmud and deleted everything that wasn’t practically binding: the rejected opinions, the digressions, the laws that only applied when the Temple stood. What remained, about a third of the original, reads like the Talmud with the verdicts left in and the noise taken out.

It was nicknamed the “little Talmud,” and it made Sefarad the world capital of legal decision. Every code in this story is built on the Rif’s foundation — and so were the people: he spent his last years heading the academy of Lucena in Spain, where his circle trained the teacher of Rambam’s father. A direct line of masters runs from the Rif straight to the giant of the next chapter.

Strategy 2: Explain it

Rashi (Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki, 1040–1105) of Troyes, France — trained in the German academies of Mainz and Worms — did the opposite. He deleted nothing. Instead he wrote a running explanation of much of the Talmud, so lucid that a motivated beginner could now enter the argument unassisted. (You’ll see a real Rashi column — and can tap it — on the sample page in the printing chapter.)

Rashi issued rulings too, but his monument is the commentary — still printed beside the Talmud’s text on every page today. He didn’t shrink the argument. He opened the door to it.

Rashi’s world did not stay peaceful. In 1096, mobs of the First Crusade, marching toward Jerusalem, massacred the Jewish communities of the Rhineland — Speyer, Worms, Mainz, the very academies that had trained him — with thousands killed, many dying as martyrs rather than accept baptism. Rashi’s last decade was shadowed by the catastrophe, and it seared something into Ashkenazi culture: the practices of the martyred communities became sacred relics. “The custom of our ancestors is Torah” — that Ashkenazi instinct we met earlier isn’t abstract piety; it’s grief, institutionalized. (The violence kept coming: Rashi’s own grandson, whom you’re about to meet, was attacked and nearly killed by Second-Crusade mobs in 1146.)

The grandchildren strike back: the Tosafot

Then Rashi’s own descendants escalated. His grandsons and their students — above all the fiery Rabbenu Tam (c. 1100–1171) of Ramerupt and his nephew Rabbi Isaac ben Samuel of Dampierre (“the Ri the Elder”) — founded the school of the Tosafot: “additions,” the most consequential set of footnotes ever written.

Here’s the method, and it’s worth savoring because it becomes the engine of everything after. Rashi explains each passage where it sits. The Tosafists read the Talmud as a single vast system: when a passage on page 40 of one tractate seems to contradict a passage in another tractate entirely, they refuse to shrug. Instead they hunt the contradiction down and resolve it with a fine distinction — here the Talmud speaks of one kind of case; there, of a subtly different one — and suddenly both passages stand, and the law has grown a new joint. Multiply that move by thousands, over four generations of scholars in dozens of towns, and you get a legal tradition of extraordinary precision, built collaboratively, argument by argument, like a coral reef.

A taste of the method A famous small example: the Talmud in one place assumes the mezuzah-style scroll passages inside tefillin are arranged in one order; elsewhere it seems to assume another. Rashi read it one way. Rabbenu Tam — his own grandson — argued the opposite arrangement. Neither side ever fully won: to this day, especially pious Jews own two pairs of tefillin, “Rashi’s” and “Rabbenu Tam’s,” and wear both. That’s the Tosafist world in miniature: total loyalty to the tradition, expressed as unflinching argument inside it — and disagreements preserved in practice, not erased.

Three more things to know about them. First, the culture: in Ashkenaz, arguing with your teacher was a form of honouring him — the Tosafot routinely conclude that grandfather Rashi got a line wrong, and no one considered this impiety. Second, the format: their notes were literally installed on the Talmud’s printed page — Rashi on one side, Tosafot facing him on the other — so that every Jew who ever opens a Talmud steps into the middle of a family argument that has been running for nine hundred years. Third, the reach: the Tosafist method did not stay French. It conquered Germany, and — carried by scholars you’ll meet shortly — it eventually transformed how Spain itself studied, which is precisely how the two worlds began to merge.

The fork in the road By 1200 two centers of gravity are visible. In Sefarad, the prestige project is the ruling: get the reader to a clear bottom line (though Spain would soon produce dazzling commentary of its own). In Ashkenaz, the prestige project is the argument itself — with practice resting on analysis plus inherited custom. Neither world lacked the other’s skill; they differed in what they treasured most. Keep this fork in mind — the endgame of this story is the page that finally holds both.

France’s last century — and its pocket codes

Christian Europe was turning. In 1240 the Talmud itself was put on trial in Paris — a converted Jew named Nicholas Donin brought charges of blasphemy before the court of King Louis IX, and leading rabbis were summoned to defend the book. They lost. In 1242, some twenty-four wagonloads of confiscated Talmud manuscripts were burned in a Paris square. Pause on what that means in a manuscript age, when every copy represented months of a scribe’s labor: much of France’s entire stock of the text went up in one fire.

French scholarship answered the way it could — by making the law portable. Two remarkable books came out of that shadow:

Sefer Mitzvot Gadol · “the Semag”

Moses of Coucy · France · completed c. 1247 · preaching tour of Spain, 1236

All practical law, filed under the traditional count of 613 commandments — and, strikingly, built by pouring Tosafist analysis into a frame openly modelled on the Rambam, whom the author quotes on nearly every page. A French Ashkenazi fusing the two worlds a full lifetime before anyone in this story reaches Toledo. Remember the Semag: it will resurface at a key moment in the print shops.

Sefer Mitzvot Katan · “the Semak”

Isaac of Corbeil · France · c. 1277

The Semag, pocket-sized — trimmed to daily essentials and famously divided into seven portions, one for each day of the week, so an ordinary householder could review the whole of practical law weekly. A lay bestseller, copied into countless prayer books across Ashkenaz. Law was escaping the scholars’ shelf.

It was a rescue operation just in time. In 1306 King Philip IV expelled the Jews of France (having first seized their property), and the world of Rashi and the Tosafists was over on its own soil. Its learning survived in exactly these portable digests — and in Germany, where the tradition’s centre of gravity had already shifted. That’s where we’ll pick up the trail.

Vc. 1180 · Egypt & Provence

The perfect code — and the war in the margins

Around 1180, the Sephardic strategy reached its logical extreme, in the hands of the most formidable mind of the Jewish Middle Ages.

The monument

He was a child of a collapsing golden age. Under the Umayyad caliphs, Córdoba had made Muslim Spain the true heir of Babylonia — courtier-rabbis, poetry, philosophy, and law flourishing together. But the caliphate shattered in 1031 into squabbling statelets, and two waves of fundamentalist Berber dynasties crossed over from North Africa to “rescue” them: first the Almoravids, then, in the 1140s, the far harsher Almohads, who offered the Jews of the cities they conquered a choice of conversion, exile, or death. When Almohad forces took Córdoba in 1148, a ten-year-old prodigy fled with his family — through years of wandering, a spell in Fez living under the persecution, and finally to the safety of Egypt.

Rambam (Maimonides)

Rabbi Moshe ben Maimon · 1138–1204 · Córdoba → Fez → Cairo

Philosopher, physician to Saladin’s court, refugee from religious persecution in Spain — and author of the Mishneh Torah, a fourteen-volume restatement of all of Jewish law: every field, including laws with no practical application for a thousand years. Perfectly organized, written in crystalline Hebrew, and — this was the scandal — with no sources cited and no dissenting opinions recorded. Just the law, stated flat.

Rambam said the quiet part out loud: he wrote that a person could now read the Torah, then his code, and need nothing in between. The Talmud’s ocean of argument, compressed into a finished monument. And what a strange, magnificent monument it is. Written across a decade (c. 1168–1178), its fourteen books open with the nature of God and the created universe and close with a vision of the messianic age — a law code framed from Creation to Redemption, with knowledge of God as the thread running through every statute. It bears the fingerprints of its world, too: like the great legal compendia of Islamic civilization around him, it opens with a book on sacred knowledge itself, and its confident systematizing belongs to the same intellectual climate that produced them. For admirers it was the greatest legal achievement since the Talmud itself — Yemenite and many eastern communities essentially adopted it as their constitution. For critics, it was an attempt to end the conversation.

Provence answers: the war in the margins

The sharpest counter-attack came from Provence — the border country that read everyone and deferred to no one.

The Raavad

Rabbi Abraham ben David · c. 1125–1198 · Posquières, Provence

The great critic of the age. He wrote glosses — pointed marginal notes — on the Rif and, most famously, on the Mishneh Torah itself, objecting line by line: here he ruled against the Talmud’s conclusion; here he follows the wrong opinion; and why has he hidden his reasoning, so no one can check his work? His tone could be brutal. His method was the point: don’t burn the code — annotate it. (He wasn’t only a demolisher, by the way: his own book on marriage and holiness, Baalei HaNefesh — “Masters of the Soul” — pioneered the idea that a law book should shape its reader’s character, not just their conduct. His neighbours called this fierce critic “the pious” and “the holy.”)

Something remarkable then happened in the print shops of later centuries: the Raavad’s attacks were published inside the Mishneh Torah, wrapped around Rambam’s paragraphs. The monument absorbed its critic and became, itself, a page of argument. Remember this move — the critical gloss, printed into the text it disputes — because in the final act, it’s exactly how Ashkenaz will get its voice into the Sephardic code that conquered the world.

Barcelona builds a bridge

Spain’s next generations pulled the traditions closer anyway — under growing Christian pressure, for by now the Reconquista had carried most of Iberia’s Jews from Muslim rule into Christian kingdoms. The bridge-builders came in a chain, beginning with Rabbi Jonah of Gerona, who ran a new kind of Spanish academy fusing French Tosafist analysis with German-style piety. Then came his great kinsman:

Ramban (Nachmanides)

Rabbi Moshe ben Nachman · 1194–1270 · Girona, Catalonia → the Land of Israel

Physician, kabbalist, Bible commentator — and the person who made the Tosafists’ French dialectic native to Spanish study halls, while defending the Rif from his Provençal critics. He repurposed the method, though: where the Tosafists argued partly for the joy of it, Ramban deployed dialectic strictly in service of the bottom line. In 1263 the crown forced him into the Barcelona Disputation, a staged debate against a Jewish convert before King James I of Aragon; he spoke so freely that, despite the king’s grudging admiration (and a cash prize), Church pressure made Spain unsafe for him, and he ended his days in the Land of Israel. First judge on Karo’s reserve bench.

The Rashba

Rabbi Shlomo ben Aderet · 1235–1310 · Barcelona

Ramban’s student, and the legal address of the age: roughly three thousand of his responsa survive, consulted on both sides of the Pyrenees. He also quietly invented something Karo would later borrow — he issued his home-and-kitchen law book Torat HaBayit in two versions: a long one with the full arguments, and a short one with just the rulings. Hold that publishing trick in mind for the finale.

A later heir of the school, the Ran (Rabbenu Nissim of Girona, c. 1320–1376), wrote what became the standard commentary printed around the Rif. The wall between the worlds was already leaking — and file these Barcelona names away, because they’ll be recalled to the bench in the final act.

The masterpiece nobody read

Provence had one more gift — and one great cautionary tale.

The Meiri

Menachem Meiri · 1249–1315 · Perpignan, on the Provence–Catalonia border

Author of the Beit HaBechirah, perhaps the era’s most serenely systematic digest of the entire Talmud: every opinion sorted by author, every conclusion stated in limpid Hebrew — the Talmud commentary the Rambam might have written, from the heart of Provence’s Maimonidean circle. He’s also beloved today for a strikingly generous legal argument: that the Talmud’s harsh rules about “idolaters” simply don’t apply to Christians and Muslims, whom he classed as “nations bounded by the ways of religion.” And this remarkable book influenced almost no one for six hundred years. Why not? Hold that question — the answer is a machine, and it arrives two chapters from now.

VI1286–1343 · Germany → Toledo

The refugee who carried Ashkenaz to Spain

In 1286, the leading rabbi of Germany, the Maharam of Rothenburg, was seized by King Rudolf I while trying to leave the country and imprisoned for ransom. By most accounts he forbade his community to pay the enormous sum — it would only teach kings to kidnap rabbis — and he died in his cell in 1293. He had seen the writing on the wall for half a century: as a young student in Paris he had watched the wagonloads of Talmud manuscripts burn in 1242, and written the lament over them that Ashkenazi communities still recite each year. His Germany was becoming unlivable.

His greatest student read the signs.

The Rosh

Rabbenu Asher ben Yehiel · c. 1250–1327 · Worms → Toledo (fled 1303)

Heir to the whole Tosafist tradition, the Rosh fled Germany in 1303 and — extraordinarily — was welcomed as chief rabbi of Toledo, the heart of Sefarad (legend says at the invitation of the Rashba himself). There he wrote a Rif-style digest of the Talmud, but with a difference: where the Rif had recorded the old conclusions, the Rosh ran the French and German analysis through them, updating the verdicts with three centuries of Tosafist argument. Ashkenazi method, Sephardic format, one book.

He never really unpacked his bags. In his writings Germany remains “our country” and Spain, to the end, “this land.” What jarred him most was the study culture: Toledo’s scholars, raised on the Rif and the Mishneh Torah, looked things up and ruled; nobody, he felt, wrestled with the Talmud itself the way his martyred teachers had. So his great digest was built deliberately as a study companion — a book you read beside the open Talmud, tracing how each ruling is earned, not a desk reference that replaces the argument. He was, in effect, a missionary for the Ashkenazi way of reading, and he largely succeeded: within a generation, Spain’s academies were in real dialogue with the Franco-German tradition.

A charming side-noteHis sons collected and organized his responsa by topic — itself a novelty. For centuries Ashkenazi rabbis had treated responsa the way we treat email: keep it while you need it, then toss it. The idea that these answers were literature worth archiving was new, and it started with his teacher the Maharam’s circle.

And then his son finished the thought.

The Tur (Rabbi Yaakov ben Asher)

c. 1269–1343 · born in Germany, wrote in Toledo · the Arba’ah Turim, “Four Columns”

“The Tur” is simply short for his book, the Arba’ah Turim — a person renamed by his own filing cabinet. A German-born son of the German master, raised in Spain, he wrote the first code that treated the two traditions as one conversation. Where his father’s digest had to be read beside an open Talmud, the Tur made a deliberate, very Spanish trade: no Talmudic citations, a use-oriented structure — a book that stands on its own. For each topic he lays out the major opinions by name — the Rif and Rambam from Sefarad, Rashi, the Tosafists and, usually last and usually decisive, “my father the Rosh” — criticizing and praising Spanish custom by turns but never demanding anyone abandon it. He was even, as far as we know, the first writer to use “Ashkenazim” and “Sephardim” as names for the two cultures: only someone straddling both would need the words. He also invented the filing system that stuck — and that deserves its own moment.

How do you file an entire civilization’s law?

This sounds like a librarian’s problem. It’s actually a power question — whoever draws the map controls how everyone after them thinks. By the Tur’s lifetime, three filing systems were competing:

  1. File it in the Talmud’s order (, ): faithful to the source — but you have to already know the Talmud to find anything, which defeats the purpose of a reference work.
  2. File it under the 613 commandments (, , ): theologically elegant — but everyday topics splinter across dozens of far-flung entries.
  3. File it by topic, for an ideal world (the ): majestic and complete — but entire volumes cover Temple sacrifices, purity rites, and the laws of kings that no one had been able to practise for a thousand years.

The Tur’s answer was ruthlessly practical: include only law that can be lived right now, and arrange it in the shape of a lived life. Four columns — the very name Arba’ah Turim comes from the four rows of gemstones on the High Priest’s breastplate — each headed with a poetic biblical phrase, and each, tellingly, written with its own reader in mind: the first for every literate householder, the second for the working community rabbi, the last two for scholars and judges. Tap each to see what it holds:

Orach Chayim“The Path of Life”Sacred time: your day and your year, from waking prayer to Shabbat and festivals.

Waking, blessings, prayer services, meals — then the weekly and yearly cycle: Shabbat, festivals, fasts. If it happens on a schedule, it’s filed here. The most-thumbed column ever since, and the one written for every literate householder.

Yoreh De’ah“It Teaches Knowledge”The holy and the forbidden: kosher food, vows, charity, conversion, mourning.

The ritual rulebook — traditionally the material a rabbi is examined on before ordination, and the column aimed at the working community rabbi.

Even HaEzer“The Stone of Help”Family: marriage, divorce, and everything between two spouses.

Betrothal, the marriage contract, and the laws of ending a marriage. The name is a wordplay: “helper” is the Bible’s word for a spouse. Written for scholars and judges.

Choshen Mishpat“The Breastplate of Judgment”Money: contracts, damages, employment, courts — Jewish civil law.

Named for the gemstone breastplate the High Priest wore when judgment was rendered. The scholars’ and judges’ column — and the one whose arrangement may echo Castile’s own great law code.

Why this version won. Scope: nothing you look up is theoretical — a book of entirely usable law, at home far beyond the academies. Findability: guess which column your question lives in, and you’re usually right. And timing: Castile was itself in a codifying mood — King Alfonso X’s great Spanish law code, the Siete Partidas, was compiled in the same land a generation earlier; the Tur is, among other things, a Jewish entry in a broader medieval moment of writing everything down. The deepest reason is in the box below.

Why the Tur matters so muchLock-in: two centuries later, Yosef Karo will choose to write his great work as a commentary on the Tur — keeping its four sections and even its chapter numbering in the Shulchan Arukh — and after that, essentially every major work of practical halakhah organizes itself the same way, down to the present. When Jews today cite a law as “Orach Chayim 271” or “Yoreh De’ah 87,” they are using the Tur’s filing cabinet. His four columns are halakhah’s street grid: the buildings have been rebuilt many times, but nobody has ever moved the streets. Before the Tur, a Spanish reader and a German reader were consulting different bookshelves. After the Tur, they were at least reading the same table of contents — with each other’s authorities cited on every page.

Meanwhile the Ashkenazi heartland kept bleeding. Another of the Maharam’s students, Mordechai ben Hillel, compiled a huge digest of Franco-German rulings — the Mordechai — and was then murdered with his family in the Rintfleisch massacres of 1298. After the Black Death pogroms of 1348–49, the centre of Ashkenazi gravity drifted decisively east, toward Poland. Hold that thought.

The merger wasn’t complete: the Tur reports the debate more often than he ends it. Ending it would take a catastrophe — and two more centuries.

VII1470s–1560s · Italy & the Ottoman Empire

The machine that changed the rules

Every book in this story so far had a ceiling: it was a manuscript. Hand-copied, wildly expensive, no two copies quite identical — and destroyable, as Paris proved in 1242, one wagonload at a time. The regional traditions were, in part, regional bookshelves: your community’s law was whatever texts your community happened to own.

Then, within a generation of Gutenberg, Jews became some of the most enthusiastic early adopters of the printing press. Hebrew presses were running in Italy by the 1470s — and it’s telling what the printers reached for first: alongside prayer books and Bibles, among the earliest Hebrew books ever printed was our friend the Semag. The press loved exactly what codes offered: organized, decided, finished texts, useful to every community at once.

The watershed came in Venice. Between 1519 and 1523, the Christian printer Daniel Bomberg produced the first complete printed Talmud — and his edition’s layout and page numbering became so universal that they have never changed since. For the first time in history, a scholar in Kraków and a scholar in Salonika were looking at literally the same page; and within a few decades, printers had added marginal cross-references wiring every Talmudic passage to the spot in the codes where it was decided. The scattered conversation of five centuries was being physically stitched together.

The cautionary tale: the Meiri Now the answer to the Meiri’s riddle. His Beit HaBechirah — arguably the era’s clearest digest of the whole Talmud — missed the press. It survived mainly in a single manuscript collection in Parma, Italy, and sat there essentially unread while printed rivals conquered the world; it wasn’t fully published until the 19th–20th centuries, too late to shape the law. In the new age there was a brutal rule: unprinted meant unheard. Influence now belonged to whoever reached the presses — a fact the two heroes of the final chapters would exploit brilliantly.

One more thread: when Spain expelled its Jews in 1492 (next chapter), the refugees carried the technology east — exiles set up the first printing press in the Ottoman Empire, in Constantinople, within a year or so of the expulsion, with Salonika close behind. So by the mid-1500s the infrastructure existed for something never before possible: one law book, physically identical, on every table in the Jewish world. All that was missing was the book.

VIII1492–1565 · Toledo → Tzfat

Catastrophe, then the Set Table

In 1492 Spain expelled its Jews. Among the refugees was a four-year-old boy from Toledo named Yosef Karo (1488–1575). His family fled to Portugal, then into the Ottoman Empire; he ended his wanderings in Tzfat (often spelled Safed), a hill town in the Galilee that had become the gathering point of the Sephardic exile — and, briefly, the most intellectually charged Jewish town on earth.

Yosef Karo

1488–1575 · Toledo → Portugal → Nikopol → Adrianople → Tzfat

Twice a refugee before the age of ten — expelled from Spain in 1492, then caught in Portugal’s forced conversions of 1497 — he rebuilt his life across the Ottoman world. In Adrianople he began the Beit Yosef (1522), a labour of some twenty years; settling in Tzfat around 1536, he sat on its rabbinic court, was among the first ordained when Rabbi Jacob Berab revived classical ordination, answered legal questions from communities across the diaspora, and wrote the Kesef Mishneh, his defence of the Rambam. From the Beit Yosef he then distilled the Shulchan Arukh — and died in Tzfat at eighty-seven, the most influential rabbi alive.

The expulsion was a century in the making. In 1391, waves of riots swept Castile and Aragon; thousands of Jews were killed and tens of thousands forcibly converted, creating a huge population of conversos living between two worlds. A staged disputation at Tortosa (1413–14) pressured thousands more to convert. In 1478 the Spanish Inquisition was founded to hunt “judaizing” among the converts. And in January 1492 Granada — the last Muslim state in Iberia — fell to Ferdinand and Isabella, completing the Reconquista; the expulsion edict against the Jews followed within weeks, with Portugal’s forced mass conversion in 1497 close behind. Eight centuries of Iberian Jewish civilization — the world of the Rif, the Rambam, the Ramban, the Rosh, and the Tur — was scattered across the Mediterranean, largely into the welcoming Ottoman Empire, whose sultans were glad to absorb the talent Spain had thrown away.

The exile scrambled everything. Spanish, North African, Greek, and Ashkenazi Jews were suddenly neighbours in the same Ottoman cities, each insisting on their own inherited practice. The old answer — “follow your local tradition” — collapses when every street holds four traditions. Karo concluded that the Jewish world needed what it hadn’t had since the Geonim: one address for the law.

The mystic behind the lawyer The most methodical legal mind of the century was also, by his own account, a visited person. Karo kept a diary — later published as Maggid Mesharim — recording decades of nighttime visitations from a heavenly mentor he understood as the personified spirit of the Mishnah, urging him on in his work and his piety. Tzfat itself was electric with this energy: it was the town of the great kabbalists, and Karo moved in their circle. He was also the star participant in the era’s most audacious legal experiment — a 1538 attempt by Rabbi Jacob Berab to revive the ancient chain of full rabbinic ordination, dormant for a millennium, as a first step toward reconstituting the supreme court of antiquity (and, behind that hope, hastening the messianic restoration; the scheme collapsed in short order over — what else — a dispute about who was ordained). Keep this in view: the drive to give the scattered Jewish people “one Torah and one law” wasn’t bureaucratic tidiness. For Karo it was a piece of cosmic repair after the catastrophe.

He began the great work in 1522, in Ottoman Adrianople, and spent roughly twenty years on the Beit Yosef — a colossal commentary that traces every single law from the Talmud through every major authority since: what the source is, who agreed, who disputed, and why. He deliberately built it on the Tur rather than on the Mishneh Torah — precisely because the Tur records the disagreements of both traditions, and Rambam’s smooth single voice would have hidden the very diversity Karo needed to adjudicate. And then, facing the thousand disputes he had so honestly documented, he needed a way to end them that didn’t depend on his own charisma. In his own telling, sitting in judgment over the giants — weighing Rashi against Rambam on the merits — would be both presumptuous and endless. His solution was almost mechanical — and that was its genius.

He even planned for its failure mode: when his court of three produces no majority — one rules yes, one rules no, and one never addressed the question — Karo’s stated fallback was to empanel a second, larger court: five more giants — the Ramban, the Rashba, the Ran, the Mordechai, and the Semag, every one of whom you’ve already met — and follow the weight of opinion among them. A court for every case; an appeals bench for every deadlock.

Karo appointed a standing Beit Din — a court — of the three greatest earlier codifiers: the Rif, the Rambam, and the Rosh (two Sephardic pillars, one Ashkenazi). His pledge: follow any two against one. And for deadlocks, a reserve bench of five. Try both:

The Beit Yosef shows the work; then, in his seventies, Karo pulled the Rashba’s old Barcelona publishing trick on a grand scale — the long version for scholars, now the short version for everyone — and distilled its conclusions into a compact book of pure rulings, aimed (he wrote) at students not yet seasoned enough to be rabbis. He called it the Shulchan Arukh — “the Set Table.” Everything decided, everything laid out, ready to eat. Two more things about it are easy to miss and worth savoring. It was born printed — there was never a manuscript era of the Shulchan Arukh, no variant copies, no scribal drift; arguably the first great Jewish book native to the machine age. And Karo left one safety valve open: where your community’s inherited practice is stricter than his ruling, he says, keep your practice — a small clause that quietly concedes the whole Ashkenazi point, as the next chapter will show.

And here the last chapter pays off. First published in Venice in 1565 — deliberately compact, designed for constant consultation rather than a scholar’s lectern — it went through printing after printing within Karo’s own lifetime, put identical copies on tables from Amsterdam to Aleppo, and quickly became the most consulted law book in the Jewish world. The Rif had needed a century to spread; the Shulchan Arukh needed a decade.

It had one enormous problem. Count the pillars again: Rif, Rambam, Rosh — two Sephardic votes to one. Run every dispute through that court and the outcomes skew systematically Sephardic, quietly overruling centuries of Franco-German practice and the sanctified customs of Ashkenaz. A table set beautifully — for someone else’s family.

IX1550s–1570s · Kraków

The tablecloth

Fifteen hundred miles north, in Kraków — the new capital of Ashkenaz — a wealthy young prodigy was writing the very same book.

The Rema

Rabbi Moshe Isserles · c. 1530–1572 · Kraków, Poland

Head of a great Kraków academy while still in his twenties, the Rema was independently working through the Tur to fix Ashkenazi law — when Karo’s Beit Yosef arrived from the south and beat him to it, magnificently. His response defined him: instead of competing, he re-cut his own research as a supplement (Darkhei Moshe), recording where the traditions of Ashkenaz — its later authorities, and above all its binding customs — diverged from Karo’s three-pillar verdicts.

Then Karo published the Shulchan Arukh itself, and the Rema made the move this whole story has been building toward. With a scholar’s wink, he introduced his project by quoting Exodus: “And Moses took the bones of Joseph with him” — Moses Isserles, carrying off the essence of the Beit Yosef (“bones” and “essence” are the same word in Hebrew). He did to Karo what the Raavad had done to the Rambam — but as partner more than prosecutor. He wrote the Mappah: “the Tablecloth,” to spread over Karo’s Set Table. Wherever Ashkenazi law or custom differed, the Rema wove a gloss directly into Karo’s text, printed from the Kraków editions of the 1570s onward in a distinct typeface, flagged with one Hebrew word meaning “gloss.” His yardsticks were the mirror image of Karo’s: where Karo counted a majority of three medieval giants, the Rema followed the principle that the law follows the later authorities — the scholars nearest his own time, who had seen all the earlier arguments — and treated entrenched custom as nearly untouchable.

Two maps of JewishnessUnderneath the two methods lay two different answers to a brand-new question. Before 1492, “Sephardic” and “Ashkenazi” mostly meant where you lived. After the expulsion scattered everyone, identity came unstuck from geography — and each drew the map differently. For Karo, the law he codified was simply the law, for all Jews everywhere. For the Rema, law travelled with peoplehood: you are Ashkenazi because of your ancestors, your customs, your language — he defined his community, strikingly, as the speakers of Yiddish and German — and Ashkenazi rulings bind that community wherever it wanders. The finished page holds both theories at once: a universal table, with a particular people’s tablecloth on it.

The effect was decisive. Within a couple of generations, “the Shulchan Arukh” meant the combined text, and it remains the reference backbone of Jewish law to this day.

One more regional nuance the fine print preserves: Poland vs. Germany. The Rema systematically favoured recent Polish-Ashkenazi practice, while old German communities along the Rhine often kept their own distinct medieval customs for centuries more, sometimes against the Rema’s rulings. Ashkenaz itself, in other words, was never quite one thing either.

X1565–1665 · Poland, Prague, the Ottoman East

The fight over the Set Table

A book doesn’t become the law of a scattered people just by being printed. For a full century after 1565, the greatest minds of the Jewish world argued — often furiously — about whether the Shulchan Arukh should win. Their objections weren’t pedantry; they went to the heart of what studying law is for.

In the Sephardic east, acceptance came fast — the book had crossover appeal to scholars and ordinary readers, Tzfat’s prestigious kabbalists embraced it, and the presses did the rest. Even there, though, the critics kept things honest: one Ottoman scholar banned his students from using the Beit Yosef as a crutch — until the day he forgot a source himself, sent a student to look it up, found it instantly in Karo’s book, and lifted the ban. An Egyptian rabbi, Jacob Castro, even wrote his own set of corrective glosses that served eastern communities much as the Rema’s served Poland, until the Mappah itself arrived and displaced them. (One community never switched at all: the Jews of Yemen stayed loyal to the Rambam’s code, as many still are.)

The real war was fought in Ashkenaz — because there, the objection was philosophical. Three camps formed:

Camp 1: Burn the whole idea (no codes, period)

Chaim ben Bezalel (1520–1588) · the Maharal of Prague (his brother) · heirs of Shalom Shachna

The Rema’s own teacher, Shalom Shachna of Lublin, had famously refused to write down his rulings at all — a judge must be free to judge each case fresh. His heirs turned that instinct into arguments that still sting. Chaim ben Bezalel’s worry was double. First, a code gives ordinary readers the illusion of understanding: they see the bottom line, think they know the law, and mislead others. Second, freezing a single answer betrays the system itself — a judge must stay free to weigh each case fresh as circumstances change, for “these and those are the words of the living God.” His brother the Maharal of Prague put it even more sharply: better to reason your way to a wrong answer through deep study than to be right by looking it up — because a ruling without its reasoning is not Torah, it’s trivia.

Camp 2: Fine, a code — but not this code

The Maharshal (Solomon Luria, c. 1510–1573) · Mordecai Jaffe, “the Levush” (1530–1612) · Yom Tov Lipmann Heller (1579–1654)

The Rema’s Kraków-born cousin and lifelong sparring partner, the Maharshal, accepted that the age needed a summary — and then indicted Karo’s on three counts: the Beit Din of Three is an arbitrary head-count, not a search for truth; it steamrolls the practice of Polish Jewry; and — a delicious irony of the print age — Karo had built on corrupted printed editions, so parts of the structure stood on typos. His alternative, Yam Shel Shlomo, re-derived everything from the Talmud itself; magnificent, enormous, and barely printed — it never had a chance. The Levush tried the middle road (rulings with their reasons, in one readable book) after being scooped out of two earlier projects — once by Karo, once by his own teacher the Rema. And Heller, last of the line, argued that the ideal code had already been written centuries before: the Rosh’s — because it keeps the receipts. After him, nobody again tried to replace the Shulchan Arukh. That silence was the sound of the argument ending.

Camp 3: Keep the code — chain it to its sources

Joshua Falk (1555–1614) · Joel Sirkes, “the Bach” (1561–1640)

The camp that actually won. Joshua Falk — a student of both the Maharshal and the Rema — accepted the Set Table but refused to let anyone eat from it blind: his commentary the Sema put each law’s sources and reasoning in the margin right beside the ruling, even marking with special signs the places where he dared to disagree with Karo, the Rema, or the Levush. Joel Sirkes did the same service for the Tur. Look at what this move really is: it resolves the thousand-year dilemma this site opened with. The bottom line and the argument, on one page — answers for the butcher, receipts for the scholar.

The arms-bearersThen history weighed in, terribly. The Chmielnicki massacres of 1648–49 devastated the Jewish communities of Ukraine and Poland — the worst catastrophe between the expulsion from Spain and the twentieth century — and, as after every catastrophe in this story, the survivors reached for consolidation. The refugee generation wrote the great commentaries that settled the matter: the Shakh (who fled Vilna in 1648) and the Taz facing each other across the page, Moses Rivkes (another Vilna refugee, writing in Amsterdam) adding the marginal source-tags of Be’er HaGolah — all of them insisting, as Falk had, that readers were free to disagree. Hebrew has a lovely name for this crowd: the nosei kelim, the “arms-bearers” — the armour-carriers who march around the king on every printed page. With them in place, even the skeptics’ demands were met: the code now travelled nowhere without its reasons.

The takeaway of the fight The Shulchan Arukh did not win because everyone surrendered. It won because its critics succeeded — their demands (show the sources, preserve the dissent, leave the judge room to differ) were absorbed into the page itself, commentary by commentary, until the objections had nothing left to point at. The final page is a peace treaty: Karo’s verdicts, the Rema’s people, and the skeptics’ receipts, bound in one binding.

XIIThe takeaway

What you now know — and where to go

You’ve just followed a legal system across a thousand years and across the globe. The toolkit you picked up:

How halakhah works as a conversation between codes and commentaries; why Sefarad codified while Ashkenaz commented and guarded custom; how the gloss — the argument printed inside the text — became the tradition’s signature move, from the Raavad to the Rema; and why printed pages from the 1570s — a Sephardic table under an Ashkenazi tablecloth — still anchor Jewish law today. Perhaps the deepest lesson: the tradition never chose between argument and answers. It found a page layout that keeps both.

  • Read the real thing — the Shulchan Arukh (with the Rema’s glosses) is free in English translation at Sefaria, alongside the Mishneh Torah, the Tur, and the Talmud itself.

Pocket glossary

Halakhah — Jewish law; literally “the way one walks.”
Talmud — the foundational record of rabbinic legal argument, completed c. 500 CE in Babylonia.
Gaon — head of a Babylonian academy, 600–1038; the era’s supreme authorities.
Responsa — written answers to legal questions sent from afar; halakhah’s case law.
Sefarad / Ashkenaz — Hebrew for Spain / Germany; the two great medieval Jewish cultural worlds.
Minhag — custom; in Ashkenaz, inherited practice with nearly the force of law.
Tosafot — “additions”; the Franco-German school of critical Talmud analysis.
Gloss — a note printed into someone else’s text; the Raavad’s weapon, the Rema’s olive branch.
Posek — a decisor: a rabbi who rules on practical law.
Shulchan Arukh — “the Set Table,” Yosef Karo’s 1565 code; with the Rema’s Mappah (“Tablecloth”), the backbone of Jewish law since.
Arba’ah Turim — the Tur’s “Four Columns” (daily life, ritual, marriage, money): still halakhah’s universal filing system and citation format.
Semag / Semak — the “Great” and “Small” Books of Commandments (France, 1200s): practical law filed under the 613 commandments; early bestsellers of Hebrew printing.
Disputation — a staged public trial of Judaism or its texts before Christian authorities (Paris 1240, Barcelona 1263, Tortosa 1413), usually with an outcome fixed in advance.
Nosei kelim — “arms-bearers”: the classic commentaries printed around the Shulchan Arukh (Shakh, Taz, Sema, Magen Avraham…), guarding the code with its sources.
Rishonim / Acharonim — “the early ones / the later ones”: medieval authorities up to roughly the Shulchan Arukh, and everyone since. Karo is often called the boundary line itself.
